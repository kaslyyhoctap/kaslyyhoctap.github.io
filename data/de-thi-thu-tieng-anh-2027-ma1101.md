## Phần 5: ĐỌC HIỂU BÀI 1 (8 câu)

**Đọc đoạn văn sau và trả lời câu hỏi từ 23 đến 30.**

---

**[I] The endowment effect**, a cornerstone of behavioural economics, describes the tendency of individuals to assign greater value to objects merely because they own them. First systematically documented by Richard Thaler in 1980, this cognitive bias has profound implications for understanding economic behaviour that traditional models — predicated on rational choice theory — failed to predict.

**[II]** Thaler's seminal experiment involved distributing coffee mugs to randomly selected participants in a classroom setting. Those who received mugs demanded approximately twice as much to relinquish them as others were willing to pay to acquire them. This disparity, termed **loss aversion**, suggests that the psychological pain of losing something substantially outweighs the pleasure of gaining an equivalent item — by a ratio some researchers estimate at nearly two to one.

**[III]** The implications extend far beyond laboratory settings. In real estate markets, homeowners frequently overprice their properties relative to comparable sales, resulting in extended time on market and eventual price reductions. In labour economics, employees may resist job offers that offer objectively superior compensation packages if accepting requires relinquishing familiar benefits or routines. The endowment effect thus contributes to **status quo bias**, a related phenomenon wherein decision-makers prefer the current state of affairs even when alternatives prove demonstrably superior.

**[IV]** Neuroscience research has begun to illuminate the biological substrates underlying these behaviours. Functional magnetic resonance imaging studies reveal that contemplating losses activates the amygdala — the brain region associated with fear and emotional processing — more intensely than contemplating equivalent gains. This neural asymmetry suggests that loss aversion may be an evolutionary adaptation: for our ancestors, the cost of losing a day's food supply far exceeded the marginal benefit of acquiring additional resources.

**[V]** Critics, however, note that replication attempts have yielded inconsistent results across cultural contexts. Cross-cultural studies suggest that the endowment effect manifests more strongly in individualistic societies than in collectivist cultures, where communal ownership norms may attenuate proprietary attachment. Some economists argue that the effect, while real, has been overstated in popular discourse, cautioning against its uncritical application to policy design. They contend that "nudge" interventions based on endowment principles may produce unintended consequences when exported to contexts with different social structures.

## Phần 6: ĐỌC HIỂU BÀI 2 (14 câu)

---

**[I] Algorithmic bias** in healthcare systems represents one of the most consequential ethical challenges of contemporary medical practice. As machine learning models assume greater responsibility for diagnostic recommendations, resource allocation, and treatment prioritisation, the potential for encoded prejudice to produce materially disparate outcomes has attracted urgent scholarly attention.

**[II]** The problem is fundamentally one of representational adequacy. Training datasets, drawn predominantly from historically privileged populations, inevitably encode the demographic skew of their sources. A diagnostic algorithm trained primarily on data from urban academic medical centres may perform poorly when deployed in rural settings serving populations with distinct genetic profiles, environmental exposures, and comorbidities. This is not merely a technical deficiency but an ethical failure with life-or-death consequences.

**[III]** Consider the widely publicised case of an algorithm used by a major healthcare system to identify patients requiring additional medical attention. Despite being race-blind — designers had deliberately excluded racial identifiers from input variables — the system systematically disadvantaged Black patients. The mechanism was subtle: the algorithm used healthcare expenditure as a proxy for medical need. Because historical inequities had resulted in lower spending on Black patients, the model inferred that they were healthier than they actually were, thereby denying them access to additional care they genuinely required. **(1)**

**[IV]** This case illuminates what scholars term **proxy discrimination**: the phenomenon whereby seemingly neutral variables carry encoded information about protected characteristics. The technical challenge of identifying such proxies is compounded by the **black box** nature of many machine learning architectures, wherein even designers cannot fully articulate the reasoning behind specific outputs. When an algorithm cannot explain itself, accountability becomes nearly impossible to establish.

**[V]** Proposed remedies fall into several categories. **Pre-processing interventions** attempt to correct biases within training data before model development begins. **In-processing techniques** incorporate fairness constraints directly into the learning algorithm itself. **Post-hoc auditing** examines model outputs after deployment to detect disparate impacts across demographic groups. Each approach carries distinct trade-offs between predictive accuracy and fairness — a tension that resists simple resolution. **(2)**

**[VI]** Yet technical solutions alone prove insufficient. Meaningful algorithmic accountability requires institutional transformation: diverse development teams capable of recognising potential biases, regulatory frameworks mandating transparency and independent auditing, and genuine patient participation in system design. Without these structural changes, technical fixes risk becoming what one researcher describes as "ethics washing" — superficial interventions that create the appearance of responsibility while preserving existing power asymmetries.

**[VII]** The stakes extend beyond individual patient outcomes. When healthcare algorithms systematically disadvantage already marginalised communities, they risk eroding public trust in medical institutions more broadly. **(3)** As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly embedded in clinical decision-making, the question is not whether algorithmic systems will shape healthcare delivery, but whether they will do so equitably or exacerbate existing disparities. **(4)**
